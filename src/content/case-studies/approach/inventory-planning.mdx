### The model

Current warehouse stock, plus projected demand over the manufacturing lead time, produces a
requirement per product. Run on a schedule rather than when somebody remembers, so the answer
exists before anyone thinks to ask for it.

The lead time is what makes this worth automating. If restocking were a purchase order, the
decision could wait until stock looked low. Because it is a production run, the decision has to
be made while the shelf still looks fine, which is precisely when nobody is thinking about it.

### Explainable beats clever

The output goes to a person about to commit money to a production run. That constrains the
design more than any technical consideration does.

A planning number that can be re-derived is one the reader can argue with — they can look at the
stock figure, look at the demand projection, and decide whether they believe it. A number that
arrived from a process nobody can follow is one they can only accept or ignore, and in practice
they ignore it and go back to the spreadsheet. A method the buyer understands and uses beats a
better method they override.

### The alternative I rejected

The obvious upgrade is a proper demand forecast — seasonality, trend, the full apparatus.

I did not build that, and I would push back on building it first. The problem being solved was
not that the existing projection was inaccurate. It was that the whole calculation lived in one
person's head and happened when they had time. Making the arithmetic automatic and repeatable
fixes that. Making it more sophisticated does not, and it costs the explainability that gets the
output used at all. Sophistication is worth adding once the simple version is being trusted and
is provably the limiting factor.

### The price monitor alongside it

A separate build, and worth naming as separate. A cloud-scheduled Python and Scrapy system
tracked competitor pricing across marketplaces to inform pricing decisions.

It shares a shape with the planner rather than sharing code: both exist so that a recurring
commercial decision stops depending on somebody remembering to go and look.
