### The shape of the pipeline

Orders arrive from two channels, are validated, are assigned a carton, are rated across
carriers, and come out the other end as a label.

Each stage can fail on its own, so each stage has to be able to hand a specific problem back to
a person rather than failing the whole run. An address that will not validate is a different
problem from a carrier that timed out, and treating them the same way means somebody has to
work out which one happened before they can do anything about it.

### Why the box is the interesting decision

Carriers do not bill on weight alone. They bill on dimensional weight, a function of the box's
volume, and charge whichever of the two is greater. So a light item in a box that is slightly
too large can cost the same as something several times heavier.

That makes carton selection the step where the money is. It has to satisfy four things at once:
the items must physically fit, the box has to be one the warehouse actually stocks, the
dimensions have to stay under the carrier's oversize thresholds, and among the boxes that
qualify it should pick whichever rates cheapest to that destination.

The last condition is the one that stops this being a lookup. The cheapest box is not a property
of the box — it depends on where the parcel is going and which carrier is carrying it, so the
candidates have to be rated rather than ranked in advance.

### The alternative I rejected

The simple version is a fixed mapping: this product goes in that box. It is easy to build, easy
to explain, and it is what most operations do by default.

I rejected it because it optimises nothing. It is the automated version of grabbing the nearest
box, and it locks in whatever the mapping got wrong on the day it was written. Enumerating the
real cartons and rating the viable ones costs more calls and answers the actual question.

### When it goes wrong

The failure cases are most of the work. Multi-item orders change the packing problem. Oversized
items fall outside the normal thresholds. Addresses fail validation for reasons ranging from a
typo to a genuinely undeliverable location.

Retries deserve particular care. The naive retry against a label endpoint buys a second label
for a parcel that already has one, which is a real cost and an operational mess. A retry here has
to be able to ask whether the previous attempt actually succeeded before it tries again.
